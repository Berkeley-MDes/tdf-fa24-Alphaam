### Asili Project Progress Report 1

### **Conceptual Exploration of the Asili Kitenge Prints Project**

#### **Motivation and Rationale**

The **"Asili Kitenge Prints Project"** is a critical response to the disparity between how culture is defined in exclusionary policies and how it is understood and lived by constituents. In East Africa, governing bodies often define culture opportunistically, tailoring its meaning to justify resource allocation, governance structures, or social policies that marginalize vulnerable communities. Constituents, meanwhile, experience culture as a rich, dynamic interplay of traditions, relationships, and daily practices. 

This project aims to visualize and make tangible the gap between these two narratives. By transforming Kitenge prints—traditional textiles with vibrant, intricate patterns and bold black boundaries—into interactive canvases, the project uses colors and patterns to represent the divergence between institutional and lived definitions of culture.

---

### **Sourcing Definitions of Culture**

1. **Governmental Definitions**:
   - **Source**: 
     - These definitions are drawn directly from laws, policies, and governance frameworks that explicitly or implicitly refer to "culture."
     - Examples include resource management policies, education systems, or governance protocols that invoke cultural norms to justify decisions.
   - **Characteristics**:
     - These definitions are often **static**, **instrumental**, and **self-serving**, crafted to suit political or economic goals.
     - The vagueness inherent in these definitions allows policymakers to frame exclusionary practices as culturally aligned, even when they contradict lived realities.

2. **Constituents' Definitions**:
   - **Source**:
     - These definitions are derived from **cultural heritage archives** that document how communities actively live and interpret culture. These archives may include:
       - Oral histories, folklore, and traditional narratives.
       - Descriptions of rituals, practices, and communal relationships.
       - Artistic expressions such as songs, dances, and crafts that embody cultural identity.
     - These archives often communicate through **colloquial** and **narrative-based formats**, capturing the fluid and contextual nature of culture.
   - **Characteristics**:
     - Constituents’ understanding of culture is **dynamic**, **diverse**, and **situational**, rooted in lived experiences rather than imposed frameworks.
     - These definitions reflect adaptability and are shaped by historical, social, and environmental contexts.

---

### **Processing the Data: Patterns and Colors**

1. **Data Processing with LLMs**:
   - To ensure parity in how cultural definitions are visualized, both institutional and lived cultural data are processed through **large language models (LLMs)**:
     - **Government Policies**:
       - Policies and laws are inputted into the LLM to identify key themes, recurring terminology, and their implicit and explicit cultural narratives.
       - These themes are then translated into specific patterns and color codes that align with the rigid, bounded nature of institutional definitions.
     - **Cultural Heritage Archives**:
       - The narrative-based data from heritage archives are processed through the same LLM models to extract thematic patterns and colors that reflect the fluid, nuanced lived experiences of constituents.

2. **Color and Pattern Mapping**:
   - **Governmental Data**:
     - Patterns are more **uniform and constrained**, echoing the black boundaries of Kitenge prints to symbolize institutional rigidity.
     - Colors are often muted or repetitive, reflecting the prescriptive and narrow lens of policy-defined culture.
   - **Constituents' Data**:
     - Patterns are more **dynamic, varied, and evolving**, symbolizing the adaptability and richness of lived culture.
     - Colors are vibrant, diverse, and overlapping, representing the complexity and intersectionality of cultural experiences.

---

### **The Black Boundaries and Enclosed Patterns**

1. **Symbolism of the Black Boundaries**:
   - The black outlines of the Kitenge prints represent the **government's working definitions of culture**:
     - Static, rigid, and unchanging frameworks that encapsulate culture as a tool for exclusionary policy justification.
     - Boundaries that constrain, limit, and define what is deemed "acceptable" within institutional narratives.

2. **Variations in the Enclosed Patterns**:
   - The colorful and evolving patterns within the boundaries symbolize the **deviation between constituents’ lived culture and institutional narratives**:
     - **Dynamic Patterns**:
       - Reflect the diversity of cultural practices, traditions, and understandings across different communities.
     - **Evolving Colors**:
       - Represent the adaptability of cultural identity in response to historical and contemporary challenges.

3. **The Interplay Between Boundaries and Patterns**:
   - The tension between the fixed black boundaries and the dynamic enclosed patterns highlights the dissonance between **institutional control** and **grassroots authenticity** in defining culture.

---

### **Key Motivations Behind the Project**

1. **Exposing Manipulation**:
   - The project sheds light on how vague definitions of culture are strategically manipulated by governing bodies to justify exclusionary policies, making them appear aligned with societal values.
   - It challenges the acceptance of such policies by visualizing the stark contrast between institutional and lived cultural narratives.

2. **Elevating Lived Experiences**:
   - By representing constituents' definitions of culture through dynamic colors and patterns, the project aims to reclaim cultural narratives from institutional control.
   - It emphasizes that culture is best defined by those who live it, not by those who seek to exploit it for political or economic gain.

3. **Creating Awareness**:
   - The visual juxtaposition of black boundaries and evolving patterns invites viewers to question the validity of institutional cultural definitions and their impact on marginalized communities.

4. **Advocating for Inclusive Policy**:
   - The project calls for policies that acknowledge and incorporate the lived realities of culture, moving away from exclusionary frameworks toward equity and representation.

---

### **Interactive Process and Visualization**

1. **Participant Interaction**:
   - Participants select colors and patterns that represent their understanding of cultural indicators such as:
     - **Community**: How do you see culture fostering connection and belonging?
     - **Governance**: What does cultural leadership mean to you?
     - **Tradition**: What practices or symbols reflect continuity in your culture?
   - Each selection alters the enclosed patterns of the Kitenge print, symbolizing the dynamic lived culture.

2. **Visual Juxtaposition**:
   - The fixed black boundaries remain unchanged, representing institutional definitions, while the vibrant patterns evolve, illustrating the gap between government and constituent perspectives.

3. **Data Integration**:
   - The processed data from both sources (policies and cultural archives) determine how patterns and colors evolve, ensuring an equal representation of both narratives.

---

### **Conclusion**

The **Asili Kitenge Prints Project** critiques the strategic vagueness and manipulative use of cultural definitions by governing bodies to justify exclusionary policies. By juxtaposing static black boundaries with evolving, vibrant patterns, the project visualizes the stark contrast between institutional and lived definitions of culture. Using Kitenge prints as a medium, it highlights the need for equitable, inclusive policymaking that reflects the authentic, diverse realities of constituents’ cultural experiences. Through this exploration, the project seeks to empower communities to reclaim cultural narratives and question policies imposed under the guise of cultural alignment.

### **This weeks's progress**

This week was dedicated to solidifying the technical foundations of my project.

Key questions guiding this week:
1. How can I accurately translate the complexity of Kitenge patterns into generative code?
2. How do I ensure the generated designs authentically represent the intricacies of Kitenge fabric?

**Exploration of Tools:**
I began researching tools and techniques for creating generative art that mimics Kitenge patterns:
- **p5.js**: Chosen for its simplicity and flexibility in generating geometric designs and dynamic visualizations.
- **Image analysis tools**: Explored ways to analyze Kitenge prints for dominant colors and patterns, using OpenCV and online color palette extraction tools like Colormind.

**Initial Coding Experiments:**
1. **Basic Shape Rendering**:
   - Experimented with simple geometric shapes (e.g., teardrops and circles) in p5.js to begin replicating common Kitenge patterns.
   - Introduced randomness to shape size and positioning to mimic the organic yet symmetrical feel of Kitenge designs.
2. **Color Integration**:
   - Started building a vibrant palette inspired by reference images of Kitenge prints, using extracted hex codes from online tools.
3. **Texture Exploration**:
   - Tested basic noise overlays to begin replicating the subtle fabric texture of printed Kitenge.

**Challenges:**
- Translating the intricate, layered patterns of Kitenge into a code-driven design while maintaining authenticity.
- Ensuring shapes appear seamlessly integrated rather than stacked on top of each other.

**State of Work:**
By the end of the week, I had:
- A draft p5.js script that generates basic teardrop shapes with random sizes and positions.
- An initial palette of vibrant colors inspired by Kitenge fabric.
- A plan for adding texture and refining the integration of shapes.
